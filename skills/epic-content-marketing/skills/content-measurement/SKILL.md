# Content measurement framework

Most content teams measure what's easy to track (pageviews, social followers, email opens) and ignore what actually matters (subscriber growth, lead quality, sales influenced by content). This skill fixes that by mapping metrics to goals instead of measuring everything and explaining nothing.

The Pulizzi framework organizes content metrics into a pyramid with four layers. The higher the layer, the more it connects to business outcomes — and the harder it is to measure. Most programs never get past the bottom two layers.

---

## Before building the measurement plan

You need to know three things:

1. What is the primary business goal for this content program? (lead generation, customer retention, sales support, awareness, revenue from content itself)
2. What is the primary content channel? (email newsletter, blog, podcast, YouTube, social, etc.)
3. What data sources does the team have access to? (Google Analytics, email platform, CRM, social analytics, etc.)

Ask these questions if the user hasn't provided them. Don't build a measurement plan for a goal the user hasn't stated.

---

## The content measurement pyramid

### Layer 1: Consumption metrics
What they measure: Are people finding and reading/watching/listening to the content?

Core metrics:
- Pageviews, unique visitors (blog)
- Email opens, open rate (newsletter)
- Podcast downloads (podcast)
- Video views, view duration (video)
- Social reach and impressions

What they tell you: Distribution and discovery are working. A platform is failing if consumption flatlines or declines month over month.

What they don't tell you: Whether anyone cares, subscribes, or buys.

### Layer 2: Sharing metrics
What they measure: Is the content good enough that people tell others about it?

Core metrics:
- Email forwards and referrals
- Social shares and saves
- Inbound links to content pieces
- Comments and replies (qualitative signal, not just count)
- Referral traffic from social

What they tell you: Content quality and audience resonance. Shares are a leading indicator of organic audience growth.

What they don't tell you: Whether readers are moving toward subscription or purchase.

### Layer 3: Lead generation metrics (subscription metrics)
What they measure: Is the content converting passive readers into known contacts?

Core metrics:
- New email subscribers (the primary metric for most content programs)
- Email list growth rate (new minus churn, expressed as a percentage)
- Subscriber sources (which content pieces or channels are driving opt-ins)
- Email unsubscribe rate (content quality signal — if unsubscribes spike after a specific issue, the content failed)
- Content-influenced form completions (if blog posts drive demo requests, track that)

What they tell you: Whether the content is building an owned audience. For most content programs, this is the most important layer.

### Layer 4: Sales metrics
What they measure: Is content contributing to revenue?

Core metrics:
- Content-influenced pipeline (deals where the contact consumed content before or during the sales cycle)
- Content-influenced revenue (closed deals with content touch points)
- Time to close for content-engaged vs. non-engaged prospects
- Customer retention rate for content subscribers vs. non-subscribers
- Upsell/cross-sell rate for subscribers

What they tell you: The business impact of content. These metrics require CRM integration and take 6–12 months of data to be reliable.

## ROO: Return on Objective

ROO replaces ROI for content marketing because most content programs don't generate direct revenue — they support revenue indirectly. ROO works by defining a specific objective, setting a target, and measuring against it.

### ROO calculation process

**Step 1: Define the objective.**
One specific, measurable outcome tied to the business goal. Examples:
- "Grow email list to 2,000 subscribers by Q4"
- "Source 20% of new demo requests from content within 12 months"
- "Reduce churn by 15% among subscribers vs. non-subscribers"

**Step 2: Set a baseline.**
Where are you starting from? No baseline, no ROO. If the team has no historical data, the first 90 days are baseline-building.

**Step 3: Set a cost.**
What does the content program cost? Include: staff time (estimate hours × hourly rate), freelancer/agency fees, tools, design, promotion spend.

**Step 4: Measure progress toward objective.**
Track the primary metric monthly. At regular intervals (quarterly is standard), compare progress to target.

**Step 5: Calculate ROO.**
If the objective is subscriber growth: did you hit the number at the cost you budgeted?
If the objective is lead influence: what percentage of closed deals had content touch points vs. the target?

There is no universal ROO formula because the objective drives the math. The value is in forcing the team to agree on what success looks like before measuring it.

## What to stop measuring

Tell the user directly: these metrics look good on dashboards and mean almost nothing.

- Total social followers (followers who don't engage are worthless)
- Raw pageviews without session quality metrics (a spike from a viral post that doesn't convert is noise)
- Time on site averages (one long session skews the whole number)
- Number of pieces of content published (volume ≠ value)
- Brand sentiment scores not tied to specific content pieces

If the user is reporting these to leadership, help them build a better report.

---

## Reporting cadence

**Weekly (internal):** Consumption and sharing metrics for recent content. Catch distribution problems early. Should take 10 minutes to review.

**Monthly (team):** All four pyramid layers. Subscriber growth rate, content-influenced leads (if tracked), any CRM data available. Compare to prior month and to goal trajectory.

**Quarterly (leadership):** ROO progress report. Are we on track to hit the objective? What's working, what's not, what changes are needed in the next quarter?

---

## Output format

Produce two deliverables:

1. **Measurement plan table** — one row per metric, organized by pyramid layer, with: metric name, data source, current baseline, target, and reporting frequency.

2. **ROO statement** — one paragraph defining the content program's primary objective, baseline, cost estimate, and how progress will be measured.

Offer to help them build the actual reporting template or connect specific tools if they share what platforms they use.

# Editorial Calendar Builder

An editorial calendar is a promise to your audience. The morning newspaper arrives on time, every morning. When it doesn't, readers notice. Your content program operates on the same principle: consistency is not optional.

This skill builds an editorial calendar that is sustainable, mapped to your strategy, and practical enough to survive contact with a real team and real deadlines.

---

## Before building the calendar

The editorial calendar is downstream from strategy. You need three things before you can build a working calendar:

1. **A content marketing mission statement** — what you publish and why
2. **An audience persona** — who you're publishing for
3. **An engagement cycle** — the stages your audience moves through from stranger to subscriber to customer

If any of these are missing, stop and build them first. A calendar without a strategy is just a list of tasks.

Ask the user for these inputs. If they have them, proceed. If they don't, point them to the `epic-content-strategy` skill.

## Phase 1: Decide frequency and format

This is the most important and most commonly mis-decided part of content planning. The default error is to commit to too much, too fast. Pulizzi's rule: consistency beats frequency. A weekly newsletter that never misses beats a daily newsletter that runs for six weeks and then stops.

Ask:
- What content type is primary? (blog posts, newsletter, podcast, video, etc.)
- Who will create the content? (solo founder, small team, freelancers, combination)
- How many hours per week can realistically go into content production?
- Have they ever run a consistent content program before? How long did it last?

Guide them toward a frequency they can actually maintain for 24 months, not just 3 months. If they want to publish 5 blog posts per week but have one part-time writer, push back. Help them find the sustainable number.

**Minimum viable consistency:** For most content programs, 1 substantive piece per week is the floor. Below that, the audience can't form a habit around you. More than 5 pieces per week without a dedicated team is usually unsustainable.

## Phase 2: Set content themes

Content themes prevent the editorial calendar from becoming an infinite list of one-off ideas. They give each week, month, or quarter a coherent thread, which makes content both easier to produce (you know the frame) and easier to consume (the audience can follow a narrative).

Ask:
- What are the 3–5 areas your audience cares most about, within your content niche?
- Which of those maps best to the business goal (lead gen, retention, awareness, etc.)?
- Are there seasonal or timely hooks relevant to your industry?

Then build a simple theme structure:

**Monthly theme system (for editorial calendars that plan by month):**
Each month gets one primary theme. All content that month stays within or connects to that theme. This doesn't mean every piece is identical — it means they share a frame that builds over time.

**Pillar topic system (for blogs and SEO-heavy programs):**
Define 3–5 pillar topics that represent the brand's subject matter authority. Each piece of content is assigned to one pillar. Pillars can rotate monthly or be persistent.

**Series structure (for newsletters and podcasts):**
Recurring segments reduce the cognitive load of planning and help audiences develop expectations. ("The Wednesday deep dive always covers one specific case study. The Friday edition always ends with a resource recommendation.") A newsletter with recurring segments is significantly easier to produce because 60% of the structure is already decided.

## Phase 3: Map the calendar

Produce a working calendar in table format. The level of detail should match the brand's needs:

**For a solo creator or small team:**
Week-by-week view, one to two months out, with theme, content type, and working headline.

**For a larger team:**
Month-by-month theme view plus a rolling two-week production calendar with assigned owner, draft due date, and publish date.

Always include:
- Publish date
- Content type
- Working headline or topic
- Audience engagement stage it serves (awareness / consideration / subscription / conversion / loyalty)
- Primary channel

**Sample weekly newsletter calendar structure:**

| Week | Theme | Content angle | Engagement stage | Headline draft |
|------|-------|--------------|-----------------|---------------|
| May 5 | [Theme A] | [Specific angle] | Awareness | "..." |
| May 12 | [Theme A] | [Follow-up angle] | Consideration | "..." |
| May 19 | [Theme B] | [New angle] | Awareness | "..." |
| May 26 | [Theme B] | [Conversion angle] | Subscription | "..." |

## Phase 4: Build the production workflow

A calendar without a production process fails within 8 weeks. Map out:

**Who does what:**
- Content brief or assignment: who creates it, by when
- First draft: who writes/records/produces, by when
- Review/edit: who reviews, by when
- Final approval: who signs off
- Publishing and promotion: who handles distribution

**Lead time rule:** The calendar should be planned 4–6 weeks ahead. Production should be 1–2 weeks ahead of publishing. Never be writing content the day it goes live.

**Buffer content:** Maintain a backlog of 2–4 pieces that are complete but not yet scheduled. These are the reserve tank when life happens (illness, competing priorities, a story that breaks and needs to go first).

---

## Phase 5: Build the idea backlog

The editorial calendar needs a continuous supply of content ideas. Build a structured idea backlog:

Sources of ideas (from the book):
- Frequently asked questions from customers or readers
- Search queries driving traffic to the site (Google Search Console)
- Comments, replies, and social engagement on past content
- Industry news or research worth opining on
- Guest contributors, interviews, or expert perspectives
- Repurposed versions of high-performing past content

Create a simple capture system: a shared doc, Notion database, or Trello board where any team member can drop ideas with a one-line description and the audience engagement stage it serves.

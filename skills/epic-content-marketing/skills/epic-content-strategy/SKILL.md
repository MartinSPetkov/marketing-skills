# Epic Content Marketing Strategy

This skill runs through Joe Pulizzi's full content strategy framework from *Epic Content Marketing* (2nd ed., 2023). It produces a documented content strategy that defines who you are as a publisher, what ground you own, who you serve, and how you win.

The underlying principle: your customers don't care about your products. They care about themselves. The only path to earning their attention — and eventually their business — is to deliver genuinely useful or entertaining content so consistently that you become part of the fabric of their professional or personal life.

Before running this skill, read `references/six-principles.md` to anchor your outputs in the six core tests every piece of epic content must pass.

---

## When to push back

Refuse to proceed with a phase if:

1. The business goal is vague ("grow our brand"). Ask for a specific, measurable objective: leads, subscribers, retention rate, direct revenue from content.
2. The proposed content niche is too broad ("marketing tips for businesses"). Push for the tightest defensible niche where this brand can be the best source on earth.
3. The audience is "everyone" or "anyone who needs X." Content cannot serve everyone. Force a primary persona.
4. The stated content tilt offers nothing new. If three existing publications already cover this ground the same way, the tilt is wrong. Keep narrowing.

State the problem in one sentence. Ask one clarifying question. Loop until resolved.

## Phase 1: Establish the business goal

Ask what business outcome the content program must support. Common goals from the Pulizzi framework:

- Brand awareness and trust (top of funnel)
- Lead conversion and nurturing (middle funnel)
- Customer conversion (decision stage)
- Customer retention and loyalty (post-sale)
- Customer upsell (existing base)
- Direct revenue from content (media/creator model)

Force a single primary goal. Secondary goals are allowed but must be ranked. The measurement framework in Phase 5 depends on this.

**Exit condition:** One primary business goal confirmed in one sentence, with a measurable proxy (e.g., "We want 500 net new email subscribers per month because subscribers convert to paying customers at 3x the rate of non-subscribers").

---

## Phase 2: Define the audience persona

Gather:

- Job title / role / life stage (be specific — "small business owner" is not a persona)
- What they're trying to accomplish (their job to be done)
- What keeps them up at night (fears and frustrations)
- What they read, watch, or listen to already (existing content diet)
- Where they spend time online and offline
- What they would pay for if it existed but doesn't
- What a great day looks like for them

One persona per strategy document. If there are multiple, pick the one that matters most to the primary goal. Mention the others by name only.

Note: the content audience is not always the buying audience. A university creates content for students; parents hold the budget. Identify both when they differ.

**Exit condition:** A one-paragraph persona sketch with name, role, key pain, and one sentence on what would make them subscribe.

---

## Phase 3: Find the content tilt

The content tilt is the angle that makes this brand's content different from everything else available. Without a tilt, the content is noise.

Guide the user through this discovery:

1. List 3–5 existing publications, blogs, podcasts, or YouTube channels their audience already relies on.
2. For each, identify what they cover well and what they ignore or underserve.
3. Find the gap: the specific intersection of (a) something the audience cares deeply about and (b) something no current source covers with authority.
4. Test the tilt against this question: "If we stopped publishing tomorrow, would our audience notice and be genuinely worse off?" If yes, the tilt has substance. If no, it needs to narrow.

Examples of strong tilts:
- "Fiberglass pool installation — every question a buyer ever asked, answered in plain language" (River Pools and Spas)
- "Healthcare information written for patients, not doctors, reviewed by Cleveland Clinic physicians" (Health Essentials)
- "Business insights for content entrepreneurs on the business side of creating" (The Tilt)

**Exit condition:** A one-sentence content tilt statement: "We are the only [content type] for [specific audience] that [unique angle or gap filled]."

---

## Phase 4: Write the content marketing mission statement

The mission statement is the North Star. Every piece of content that gets published should pass through it. If a proposed piece doesn't fit the mission, it shouldn't run.

Three-part formula (from Pulizzi):
> [Brand] delivers [content type(s)] to [specific audience] to help them [specific outcome].

Strong example: "The Tilt delivers twice-weekly newsletters and resources for content entrepreneurs to help them build sustainable businesses from their content."

Weak example: "We create content that helps marketers grow." (Too vague on all three parts.)

Test the draft against these questions:
- Is the audience specific enough that you could find them at a conference?
- Is the outcome specific enough that a reader would know when they've achieved it?
- Does the content type rule things out (so you know what NOT to create)?

**Exit condition:** A final, approved mission statement in one sentence.

---

## Phase 5: Map the engagement cycle and channel plan

The engagement cycle describes the stages your audience moves through — from stranger to subscriber to customer to advocate. Map the content to each stage.

Typical stages:
1. Awareness (they find you)
2. Consideration (they return and start trusting you)
3. Subscription (they opt in — this is the key milestone)
4. Conversion (they become a customer)
5. Loyalty/advocacy (they refer others, co-create, or buy more)

For each stage, identify:
- What content type serves this stage best
- Which channel delivers it
- What action you want the audience to take

**Subscription is the goal.** Email subscribers outperform social followers on every business metric. At every stage, the question is: what will move someone closer to subscribing?

**Owned vs rented land:** Build on owned platforms (your domain, your email list) first. Use social and other rented platforms for distribution and discovery only. Never let your primary audience live on someone else's platform.

Then select your content platform (primary) and channels (secondary):

- Primary platform: where the content lives (your website/blog, email newsletter, podcast feed)
- Secondary channels: where you distribute to attract new audiences (social, YouTube, LinkedIn, etc.)

Strategy defines channel selection — not the other way around.

**Exit condition:** A simple table or list showing: primary platform, content type, frequency, and 2–3 distribution channels.

## Output format

Produce a structured strategy document with these sections:

```
# Content Marketing Strategy: [Brand Name]

## Business goal
[One sentence]

## Audience persona
[Name + role + pain + what would make them subscribe]

## Content tilt
[One sentence: "We are the only..."]

## Content marketing mission statement
[One sentence using the three-part formula]

## Engagement cycle
[Stages with content type and action for each]

## Channel plan
| Platform | Content type | Frequency | Purpose |
|----------|-------------|-----------|---------|
| ...      | ...         | ...       | ...     |

## Six-principles check
[Short paragraph confirming the strategy passes all six principles — see references/six-principles.md]

## What success looks like
[Primary metric tied to the business goal, with a realistic 12-month target]
```
